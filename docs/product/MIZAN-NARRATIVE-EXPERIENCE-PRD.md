## 1. Product decision

Mizan's first public experience must make one change in the visitor's mental model:

> AI safety is no longer only about controlling what a model says. Once an agent can use tools,
> enterprises must control what that software actor is allowed to do.

Mizan is the control boundary for that transition.

The experience must show, rather than merely claim, that Mizan can sit between an AI agent and its
tools, decide whether a proposed business action is allowed, denied, or requires human authority,
bind any approval to the exact action, and produce evidence that another party can inspect.

This PRD starts with the impression Mizan must create and works backward into product requirements.
It does not prescribe the website framework, animation library, deployment architecture, analytics
vendor, or design implementation. The delivery teams own those choices.

---

## 2. Why this exists

The current product has unusually deep primitives—contextual authorization, approval epochs and
quorum, request-bound execution authority, evidence chains, external timestamping, and two offline
verifiers—but a new visitor will not infer that value from a generic control-plane dashboard.

The failure modes are predictable:

- “AI governance” sounds like a policy repository.
- “AI security” sounds like prompt filtering, red teaming, or another firewall.
- “Authorization” sounds like ordinary IAM or API access control.
- “Human in the loop” sounds like a workflow product.
- “Audit evidence” sounds like logs generated by the same system asking to be trusted.
- A long feature inventory hides the single control loop that makes Mizan understandable.

The public experience must resolve these category confusions in the correct order.

## 3. Product truth and ambition

### 3.1 The present-tense product

For a governed integration, Mizan can:

1. receive a proposed tool action before execution;
2. evaluate agent, principal, intent, tool, resource, arguments, policy, and risk context;
3. return `ALLOW`, `DENY`, or `REQUIRE_APPROVAL`;
4. route a consequential action through controlled approval;
5. issue short-lived execution authority bound to the approved request;
6. record decision and execution events;
7. export an evidence bundle that two offline implementations can check.

The exact shipped/preview/absent status remains governed by `docs/product/MODULE_LEDGER.md`. No public
surface may silently broaden that ledger. Since 2026-08-30 the production custody (Vault Transit) and
durable evidence (S3 Object Lock) gates are closed; workforce login, install, restore and the
full-journey production gate are not (`MIZAN-COMMERCIAL-STRATEGY.md` §2).

### 3.1a Products in the public story

| Product | Place in the front door | Maturity label shown |
|---|---|---|
| **Mizan** | The whole story: hero, scenario, approval lab, evidence lab, architecture. | Technical Preview |
| **Memtara** | One branch of the regulated use case (catalogue UC-2): a client's device proves suitability and the recommendation tool cannot run without the proof. Appears only on the use-case page and as Wow 6, and only with its own maturity label. Mizan verifies the short-lived proof token, binds its suitability result and product ISIN into policy, and cross-anchors it in the exported record. | Technical Preview |
| **AIHOOTS** | Absent. Not a product, not a diagram element, not a footnote. | — |

### 3.2 The product destination

Mizan intends to become the enterprise control plane through which autonomous software actors are
identified, constrained, supervised, observed, and made accountable across their lifecycle.

The destination contains three planes:

```text
BEFORE ACTION                  AT ACTION                     AFTER ACTION

Discover and design     ->     Decide and enforce     ->     Prove and improve

agent inventory                trusted identity               decision records
tool and data map              contextual policy              independent verification
authority design               risk and threat signals        investigation and replay
policy simulation              human authority                control effectiveness
deployment review              execution binding              autonomy progression
```

The first public experience leads with the **At Action** wedge and uses Before/After to show how the
platform can extend. It must not imply that all destination capabilities are available now.

### 3.3 The differentiating thesis

Mizan must not claim to be the only company governing agent actions. Identity providers, AI security
platforms, gateways, policy engines, and newer control-plane products overlap this space.

The thesis to test is narrower and stronger:

> For regulated, consequential workflows, technical buyers will value one continuous chain from
> action context, to policy decision, to the right human authority, to exact-request execution, to
> independently checkable evidence.

The “wow” is the integrity of the whole chain—not ownership of any one component. This remains a
commercial hypothesis until external users choose, test, and pay for it.

- CISOs and CIOs looking for the category and enterprise impact;

The first experience is not optimized for casual consumer AI users, broad “future of AI” traffic,
or teams building content-only chatbots with no consequential tool access.

## 5. Required audience outcomes

The experience is successful only if an unbriefed technical visitor can reach these states:

| Time | Required outcome | Test method |
|---|---|---|
| 10 seconds | Understand that Mizan controls AI-agent actions, not model answers. | Unprompted five-second recall after leaving the hero. |
| 30 seconds | Repeat the core sequence: propose, decide, approve if needed, execute, prove. | Moderated comprehension test. |
| 2 minutes | Locate Mizan between an agent and enterprise tools and distinguish it from IAM, guardrails, and observability. | Architecture sorting task. |
| 5 minutes | See one safe action flow, one consequential action pause, and one prohibited action fail. | Guided or self-guided scenario completion. |
| 10 minutes | Inspect why a decision occurred and what exact action was approved. | Evidence/decision record task. |
| 15 minutes | Verify or falsify an exported evidence claim without trusting the UI. | Download, alter, and run verifier task. |
| 20 minutes | Understand what exists today, what is preview, and what is future. | Capability classification test. |

The experience must be usable in three modes:

- **self-guided:** a technical visitor with no Mizan operator present;
- **guided:** a seven-minute first meeting or event demonstration;
- **deep review:** a 30–45 minute architecture/security session.

## 6. The story

### 6.1 Category-opening line

> Your AI agent can already reach the tool. The unanswered question is whether this action should
> be allowed to happen now, on this resource, for this user.

### 6.2 Product line

> Mizan controls consequential AI-agent actions before execution and produces proof after.

### 6.3 Thirty-second story

AI guardrails inspect what a model says. IAM determines whether an identity can reach a system. But
an enterprise agent may have legitimate access and still propose the wrong action, with the wrong
arguments, under the wrong context. Mizan evaluates that action at the tool boundary. Safe work can
continue, prohibited work is denied, and consequential work waits for the right human authority.
Any granted authority is bound to the exact request, and the resulting decision can be independently
checked later.

### 6.4 Seven-minute story

The primary scenario is a regulated financial workflow because it makes authority, context, and
consequence immediately visible.

1. A relationship manager asks an agent to reduce a customer's equity exposure.
2. The agent reads the permitted portfolio and risk-profile data. Mizan allows the reads.
3. The agent proposes a rebalance. Mizan explains why the action is consequential and pauses it.
4. The designated supervisor sees the business action, changed fields, affected customer, policy,
   risk, and scope—not an opaque tool-call identifier.
5. The supervisor approves the exact proposal. Mizan issues bounded, one-use execution authority.
6. The tool executes once and returns an outcome. Reuse or mutation of the request is rejected.
7. Untrusted content then instructs the agent to export customer portfolios. The policy boundary
   denies the action; the content does not redefine authority.
8. The presenter exports the decision evidence, alters one material field, and runs a verifier. The
   unaltered bundle passes and the modified one fails.

Closing line:

> We did not merely record what the AI said. We controlled what it was allowed to do—and made that
> decision checkable outside Mizan.

**Wealth-desk variant (UC-2; Technical Preview).** Between steps 2 and 3, the copilot's proposed
recommendation requires a suitability proof. The client's device produces it against the bank's
registered product terms and discloses one bit; Mizan verifies the token and only then evaluates the
order. The closing line gains one clause: *"—and the bank never held the client's figures."* The live
Technical Preview is driven by `scripts/demo_memtara_walk.py`; the committed transcript is the backup.

### 6.5 Objection path

The visitor must be able to branch from the main story into the challenge most relevant to them:

| Objection | Required answer surface |
|---|---|
| “This is just IAM.” | Show valid identity and tool access producing different decisions when action arguments, resource, intent, or risk changes. |
| “This is just an AI firewall.” | Show that content inspection is an input, while the governed object is a business action and its authority to execute. |
| “This is just approval workflow.” | Show approval bound to canonical request details, authority epoch/quorum, expiry, one-use execution, and mutation rejection. |
| “This is just logs.” | Export the bundle, disconnect from Mizan, verify it, then tamper with it and observe failure. Disclose the remaining trust boundary. |
| “The agent can bypass it.” | Identify the enforcement boundary, credential placement, governed/ungoverned paths, and the deployment property required to prevent direct tool access. |
| “It will add too much friction.” | Demonstrate safe reads flowing automatically and controls becoming stronger only as consequence rises. |
| “We can build policy middleware.” | Compare the full lifecycle: context trust, approval authority, TOCTOU binding, receipts, evidence, verification, operations, and testing. |
| “What if Mizan is unavailable?” | State the fail-closed/degraded-mode contract actually supported; do not improvise availability claims. |

## 9. The five required wow moments

### Wow 1 — one field changes authority

The same authenticated agent proposes the same tool with a material argument changed. A safe read or
small action is allowed; a consequential variant pauses or denies. The visitor sees that Mizan is not
making a coarse “agent can call tool” decision.

**What this proves:** contextual, action-level authorization.
**What it does not prove:** perfect intent inference or universal risk classification.

### Wow 2 — approval is not reusable permission

A supervisor approves one exact rebalance. The approved call executes once. A changed destination,
amount, customer, or replayed token fails.

**What this proves:** approval and execution are bound across the time-of-check/time-of-use gap.
**What it does not prove:** the downstream business system itself is correct.

### Wow 3 — safe autonomy continues

Permitted reads and bounded low-consequence work proceed without a person. Only the action crossing a
defined authority boundary pauses.

**What this proves:** control need not mean approval fatigue.
**What it does not prove:** a production latency or availability SLA until measured.

### Wow 4 — instructions cannot grant authority

Untrusted content tells the agent to ignore policy and export sensitive records. The agent may form
the tool proposal; the external policy boundary still denies it.

**What this proves:** tool arguments cannot cross the tested policy namespace and give themselves
authority.
**What it does not prove:** a shipped prompt-injection detection engine or DLP capability.

### Wow 5 — the audience attacks the evidence

The visitor downloads the bundle and verifies it offline. They modify or remove an event and see the
check fail. The screen also says what the verifier cannot establish.

**What this proves:** portable integrity checks with explicit limits.
**What it does not prove:** “tamper-proof,” complete non-omission, or independence from every key holder.

### Wow 6 — the bank never held the figures *(Technical Preview)*

A recommendation tool is policy-bound to a suitability proof. The client's device proves, against the
bank's registered product terms, that income, liquidity, risk tolerance and concentration were assessed
and discloses one bit. Mizan verifies the proof offline and gates the action on it. The compliance
officer opens **one** bundle and sees the decision, the proof hash and both chain heads — and sees that a
decline is recorded identically to an approval.

**What this proves:** a privacy-preserving predicate can be a first-class policy input with a
regulator-re-verifiable record.
**What it does not prove:** regulatory compliance, revocability of a proof inside its 300-second life,
or anything about circuits other than suitability. Never say “18-month ZKP head start.”

## 10. Technical content model

The experience must use a stable vocabulary:

| Object | Required plain-language definition |
|---|---|
| Principal | The person or workload on whose behalf the agent acts. |
| Agent | The software actor proposing an action. |
| Intent | The declared task or purpose providing decision context. |
| Tool | The bounded enterprise capability the agent wants to invoke. |
| Resource | The specific customer, account, environment, record, or asset affected. |
| Action | The canonical proposed operation and material arguments. |
| Policy | The versioned rule set that evaluates the action context. |
| Decision | Allow, deny, or require human approval, with reasons. |
| Approval | Time-bounded human authority for the exact proposal, not a general entitlement. |
| Execution authority | Short-lived capability permitting the approved action to execute under its binding. |
| Receipt | The recorded execution attempt and outcome. |
| Decision record | The joined history of context, policy, decision, approval, execution, and outcome. |
| Evidence bundle | Portable records and proofs that can be checked outside the operating UI. |

Raw implementation terms may appear in deep documentation, but the main story must not require a
visitor to understand epochs, leases, outboxes, RFC 3161, or canonicalization before understanding
the business control.

## 11. Look and feel requirements

The experience should feel like an institutional evidence control room: calm, exact, legible, and
comfortable displaying both passing and failing states.

### Required qualities

- confidence through evidence, not neon or superlatives;
- dense enough for experts, with progressive disclosure for first-time visitors;
- action/resource/risk language before opaque IDs;
- semantic green, amber, and red reserved for actual state;
- no decorative “production,” “secure,” or “verified” badges;
- visible runtime, identity/session, tenant/demo, and evidence-coverage state;
- diagrams that remain readable on mobile and when printed;
- keyboard access, visible focus, reduced-motion support, and WCAG 2.2 AA target;
- downloadable assets that retain provenance, date, and version.

### Prohibited presentation patterns

- a generic hacker terminal aesthetic as the primary brand expression;
- dashboards filled with invented activity to imply production adoption;
- animated network graphs with no explanatory or decision value;
- undifferentiated feature clouds;
- customer or regulator logos without written permission;
- a green system state derived from static markup;
- risk colors used as decoration;
- hidden limitations or roadmap footnotes that contradict the hero.

## 14. Claim maturity model

| State | Meaning | Public language |
|---|---|---|
| Shipped | Code exists, has a production caller, and is exercised by a meaningful test. | “Mizan does…” with scope and version. |
| Technical Preview | Reachable and demonstrable but missing at least one pilot-grade operational or assurance gate. | “Available in technical preview…” plus limitation. |
| Roadmap | Problem and intended direction are accepted; delivery is not committed. | “We plan to explore/build…” with no date unless approved. |
| Research | A hypothesis, experiment, or proposed control awaiting validation. | “We are testing whether…” |
| Absent | Not implemented and not approved as roadmap. | Do not imply; answer directly if asked. |

Any content item that mixes states must label them at the point of the claim, not only in a global
disclaimer.

## 15. Launch gates

### Gate A — narrative comprehension

- 8 of 10 target users identify action control as the problem;
- 7 of 10 reproduce the five-step control loop;
- 7 of 10 correctly distinguish Mizan from prompt guardrails, IAM, and logging;
- no more than 1 of 10 mistakes roadmap for shipped product.

### Gate B — end-to-end product proof

- safe, approval-required, and denied paths pass from the public scenario;
- changed/reused/expired execution authority visibly fails;
- exported bundle passes both offline verifiers;
- materially altered bundle fails;
- demo copy matches current module-ledger status;
- all business data and downstream effects are identified as synthetic/simulated;
- if the wealth-desk variant is shown, the Mizan→Memtara token verification is merged code exercised by
  CI (T-133), not a narrated header — otherwise the variant is labelled a recording of two consoles.

### Gate C — independent technical use

- a person outside the build team completes the scenario from a clean environment;
- that person downloads, verifies, tampers with, and rechecks the bundle;
- they can state at least two trust assumptions or limitations;
- all undocumented recovery help is treated as a launch defect.

### Gate D — operational safety

- authentication and session state are appropriate for the selected exposure level;
- public sessions and data are isolated;
- abuse limits and reset behavior are tested;
- status and degraded modes are truthful;
- security disclosure and incident ownership are assigned.

### Gate E — commercial readiness

- evaluation offer, qualification criteria, and owner are published;
- response SLA for technical inquiries is staffed;
- the white paper and initial editorial sequence are approved;
- CRM/content attribution can separate curiosity from qualified evaluation intent;
- no paid promotion begins before Gates A–D pass.

## 17. Stop and correction rules

- If experts consistently interpret Mizan as prompt security, simplify the front door before adding
  more capabilities.
- If the three decision paths require presenter explanation, the interactive proof is not launch-ready.
- If external reviewers will not run the verifier, do not keep calling independent verification the
  primary differentiator; apply `docs/product/FALSIFICATION_TESTS.md`.
- If only audit teams show urgency, revisit sponsor, pricing, and value framing before increasing lead
  generation.
- If action-level control is valued but the evidence lab is not, move evidence from hero to assurance
  layer rather than forcing the thesis.
- If a competitor makes the same claim, remove exclusivity language and compare observable control
  properties.
- If the product cannot safely expose a self-guided environment, publish a recorded proof and
  downloadable lab until the exposure gates close.

## 18. Delivery sequence

The implementation team should estimate and sequence the work after definition review. Product does
require the following dependency order:

1. **Truth foundation:** claim registry, maturity states, source artifacts, vocabulary, limitations.
2. **Narrative prototype:** story, information architecture, low-fidelity comprehension testing.
3. **Proof prototype:** one deterministic scenario and evidence lab tested with outsiders.
4. **Visual system:** institutional look and responsive interaction built around real states.
5. **Technical center:** architecture, security, integration, evidence, and future-map content.
6. **Content launch:** paper, LinkedIn sequence, community artifacts, technical evaluation offer.
7. **Promotion:** only after narrative, product, independent-use, and operational gates pass.

Parallel work is encouraged inside a stage where dependencies permit it. Visual polish must not freeze
before narrative comprehension and state truth are validated.

## 19. Definition review questions

The cross-functional team must answer these before committing implementation:

1. Which exact release and deployment class will the public proof represent?
2. Which parts of the scenario are real Mizan paths, synthetic customer data, and simulated tool effects?
3. What is the supported bypass-prevention deployment pattern for that proof?
4. What trusted sources provide principal, agent, resource, intent, and risk context?
5. Which approval identity and step-up controls are required for public versus private evaluation?
6. Which evidence properties are verified, externally anchored, or still trust-dependent?
7. What happens when Mizan, the policy engine, approval service, evidence store, or TSA is unavailable?
8. What data may public visitors submit, and how is it isolated and deleted?
9. What public artifact can an evaluator run without an account or sales conversation?
10. Which measurable result would cause us to change the story rather than add more copy?

## 20. Definition of done

This PRD is complete only when the shipped experience allows an independent technical expert to:

1. describe the problem and Mizan's boundary accurately;
2. observe all three decision paths on one coherent use case;
3. explain why the consequential action needed human authority;
4. prove that approval cannot authorize a changed or repeated request;
5. inspect the decision and execution outcome;
6. verify and then falsify a portable evidence bundle outside the Mizan UI;
7. identify what is shipped, preview, roadmap, and absent;
8. understand the principal trust assumptions and failure behavior;
9. see how the action-control wedge extends into the long-term platform;
10. choose a concrete next step: self-evaluate, request architecture review, or propose one controlled
    agent pilot.

The final impression should be simple:

> Mizan gives autonomous software less ambiguity about authority and enterprises more evidence about
> what actually happened.
